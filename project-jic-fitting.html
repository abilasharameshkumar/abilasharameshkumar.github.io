Why This Part

A JIC 37 degree flare fitting is one of the most common connectors in hydraulic systems. It looks simple, but designing one well means getting the threads, the sealing cone, and a set of tight tolerances all correct at once. That made it a strong piece to model and fully document.

I designed a size -8 fitting, which connects half inch tube and uses a 3/4-16 straight thread, for a system rated around 3000 psi.

How the Seal Works

The most important thing about a JIC fitting is where the seal happens. It is not the threads. The seal is metal on metal between the 37 degree cone on the male part and the matching seat inside the female part. The threads only pull the two parts together.

Once I understood that, every design and tolerance decision pointed back to protecting that one cone surface.

Modeling and Assembly

I built two parts in Inventor. The male fitting has the sealing cone, the straight thread, a wrench hex, and a through bore. The female mate has an internal thread, an internal 37 degree seat, and the same bore. Each part started as a 2D profile that I revolved into a solid, with the hex cut as a separate feature and cosmetic threads applied.

I then brought both parts into an assembly and seated the cones together. A section view through the assembly confirms the two cones meet and the bores line up into one channel.

Tolerancing and GD&T Strategy

The drawing follows ASME Y14.5-2018. Tolerances are tight only where the seal needs them and loose everywhere else, which keeps inspection and scrap costs down.

I used total runout rather than concentricity because ASME Y14.5-2018 removed concentricity as a symbol.

Designing for Manufacturing

The part is built for high volume CNC turning. The biggest manufacturing decision was starting from hex bar stock instead of round bar. This produces the wrench flats straight from the raw material, so there is no separate milling step and no second setup. Fewer setups is the largest lever on cost at volume.

A relief groove before the thread lets the thread run out cleanly and lets the nut seat fully.

Choosing the Material

I specified 316 stainless steel. It resists corrosion well, including in wet and marine conditions, and it holds strength across a wide temperature range. That suits a sealing part where a leak is not acceptable.

A plated carbon steel version would cost less to make, but it gives up corrosion life, so it only makes sense in dry, controlled conditions.

Deliverables
  • Inventor part and assembly files.
  • A dimensioned, GD&T annotated 2D drawing exported to PDF.
  • A one page design rationale covering design intent, material choice, and manufacturing reasoning.
